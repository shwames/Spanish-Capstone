01

Argumentative paper

The power of poetic devices in Quevedo's “¡Ah de la vida!”

This essay argues that the sonnet's ordered form intensifies its chaotic reflections on time and mortality. It supports that reading through analysis of the poem's structure, paradoxes, metaphors, and progression.

SPAN 330 · February 2025Download paper ↓
Read my reflection

I chose this essay because it gives a clear example of making an interpretive claim and supporting it through close reading. The paper argues that Quevedo's tightly ordered sonnet form creates tension with its unsettling ideas about time, death, and the instability of human life. I do not simply summarize the poem; I explain how its structure and language shape its meaning. The essay follows that claim through the sonnet's form, paradox, metaphor, and progression of ideas. For example, I discuss the paradox of being both present and already a “difunto,” and I connect the image of life as a passing river or shadow to the poem's concern with time. Those examples show why I selected this artifact for the argumentative section: it demonstrates an effort to defend a specific reading of a literary work with evidence from the text.

Working on this essay helped me practice the steps involved in building an argument in Spanish. I had to decide what the poem was doing beyond its topic, state that interpretation in a thesis, choose details that supported it, and explain how each detail connected to the larger claim. I also practiced using literary vocabulary to describe form and rhetorical choices. This matters because an academic argument needs more than an opinion; the writer has to make the reasoning visible to readers who may interpret the poem differently. The essay gave me practice explaining why a formal feature matters instead of naming it and moving on.

As a Spanish major at BYU, I have developed more confidence reading literary texts closely and discussing how their language works. I am learning to notice structure, sound, imagery, and ambiguity, then turn those observations into a focused interpretation. Writing about Quevedo also made me think about how Spanish academic vocabulary can help me express an idea precisely. At the same time, this artifact reminds me that my writing can continue to improve. I want to check spelling and grammar carefully, use consistent names and accents, and make sure every quotation is accurate and clearly integrated. I also want to distinguish more clearly between what the poem explicitly says and what I infer from it, so that readers can follow the evidence behind my interpretation.

I plan to keep developing these skills by writing more analyses of Spanish-language literature and by revising each paper for both argument and language. In future work, I want to make my thesis even more specific, organize paragraphs so each one advances a distinct part of the claim, and use sources or historical context when they genuinely strengthen the reading. I also want to consider alternative interpretations instead of treating my own as the only possible one. Quevedo's poem is useful for that practice because its images and paradoxes invite more than one response. This essay represents an important step in learning to make a literary argument in Spanish: I selected evidence, connected form to meaning, and tried to guide an academic reader through my reasoning. I will build on that foundation by making my claims more precise and my Spanish more polished. I also want to keep improving my Spanish before law school so I can communicate clearly with Spanish-speaking people in my future legal work.

02

Research paper

Mixtec, Spanish, and English in a missionary context in California

This SPAN 429 paper synthesizes sociolinguistic research and historical context to examine language contact, identity, and power in San José and Salinas.

SPAN 429R · April 2026Download paper ↓
Read my reflection

I chose my SPAN 429 paper, “El Mixteco, el Español, y el Inglés en un Contexto Misionero en California,” for the research section because it brings together course concepts, published scholarship, and observations from my mission experience in San José and Salinas. Its central claim is that describing these communities simply as Spanish-speaking misses important differences in language, power, and belonging. The paper is organized by ideas: regional varieties, language contact, language ideologies and identity, and historical context. That organization let me connect multiple sources to a single question rather than presenting a sequence of unrelated summaries. I draw on work about code-switching, communicative competence, language contact, and migration history to interpret the situations I encountered. I selected it because it shows my effort to synthesize research and use it to understand a complex language environment.

Writing this paper helped me practice finding relationships among sources. Researchers such as Gumperz, Hymes, Zentella, and Thomason and Kaufman study different aspects of language, but their ideas can be put in conversation to explain why multilingual communication is patterned and socially meaningful. I also connected sociolinguistic frameworks to the history of Mexican migration and to the presence of Mixtec-speaking communities. That required me to introduce concepts in a way that supported my analysis and to give credit to the scholars whose work shaped it. I learned that synthesis is not just including many citations. It involves explaining how evidence from different sources contributes to the paper's purpose, where ideas overlap, and what each perspective helps a reader see.

The project also changed how I think about language learning and communication. Before, I was more likely to measure Spanish against a classroom standard. The experiences described in the paper pushed me to consider how language choices depend on community, audience, history, and power. In particular, the discussion of Mixtec-speaking residents helped me see why the phrase “Spanish-speaking community” can hide important distinctions. I had to consider the limits of my own perspective as a learner and missionary, and the difference between observing an interaction and fully understanding the experiences of the people involved. That reflection is part of the research process: my experience gives the paper a starting point, but it does not replace careful sourcing or justify broad conclusions about every speaker.

As a Spanish major at BYU, I have developed skills in connecting linguistic ideas to real contexts, organizing a longer research paper, and using sources to support an analysis. I have also gained a stronger awareness that Spanish is not a single uniform system and that language practices are connected to identity and social relationships. This artifact shows work I am proud of, while also giving me goals for improvement. In future research, I want to verify each source's details, check that every factual claim is supported by the most relevant evidence, and make the distinction between my observations and established research especially clear. I also want to strengthen transitions so readers can see why I move from one concept or historical point to the next.

I plan to continue developing these abilities through research that includes perspectives from the communities being discussed and through more careful attention to the limits of any one study or experience. I want to read scholarship in Spanish and English, compare how researchers frame the same issue, and keep improving my citation and source-integration skills. This paper represents an important stage in that development because it asks me to combine personal experience with academic research without treating them as interchangeable. It demonstrates how I can use multiple sources to build a connected interpretation, and it gives me a foundation for future work on multilingualism, language policy, and the social lives of Spanish and Indigenous languages. As I prepare for law school, I want to keep improving my Spanish so I can work more effectively with Spanish-speaking people.

03

Cultural experience

Study abroad in Mexico: learning to write Maya characters

This class project represents the message that James and Willow are married on August 22, according to the interpretation from class.

Reflection · 634 words

I chose this artifact because it connects my study abroad in Mexico with a class in which I learned about Maya writing. The image is a modern class project, not an ancient inscription. According to the interpretation from class, it communicates that James and Willow are married on August 22. I wanted to include it because it shows me trying to use a writing system I had previously encountered mainly as unfamiliar shapes. It also gave me a way to think about how names, relationships, and dates can be represented in writing. The project is personal in its message, but it also led me to ask broader questions about the history and cultural context of Maya writing.

The class helped me see that Maya hieroglyphs are not simply pictures that can be guessed at by looking. The Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian describes the system as combining signs for whole words with signs for syllables, and notes that interpreting a sign depends on the language represented. That changed how I approached the page: each mark could have a linguistic role, and the arrangement mattered. Working through the assignment required close visual attention, patience, and a willingness to follow what we were taught instead of assuming I could read the symbols intuitively. I developed a better sense of how writing can combine visual design and language, and how learning even a small part of a script requires context and instruction.

The artifact also helped me consider what writing can do socially. Historical Maya inscriptions appear on monuments, buildings, ceramics, and books; they preserve dates and information about people and events. The British Museum's overview notes that some inscriptions record lineages and marriages as well as political events. My class project is not equivalent to those historical records, but its subject made that connection feel concrete: a written record can mark a relationship and place it in time. I also learned to distinguish the personal meaning of my assignment from the meanings of historical objects. I should not claim that my modern drawing reproduces an ancient Maya wedding inscription or that it represents every Maya community or language.

The historical context matters because Maya writing has not existed outside of political power. The Smithsonian explains that many Maya books were destroyed during the Spanish conquest, while surviving Maya communities continue to carry languages, traditions, and knowledge today. Reading about this history made me think about the difference between studying an ancient script and treating Maya culture as something that ended in the past. Maya people are living communities, not just subjects of archaeology. My time in Mexico gave me a setting in which to encounter this cultural material, but one class project cannot make me an expert or stand in for the perspectives of Maya people themselves. It can, however, encourage me to learn with more care and to recognize the histories behind the forms I study.

As a Spanish major at BYU, I developed skills in observing a visual text, attending to the relationship between a sign and its language, and placing a cultural artifact in historical context. I also practiced questioning my first impressions and separating what I learned in class from what I can independently claim about the script. I chose this piece because it represents a moment when language learning expanded beyond Spanish and helped me notice the complexity of Indigenous writing traditions in Mexico. I want to continue learning about Maya history and contemporary Maya communities, and to listen to scholars and community members whose expertise is grounded in those traditions. In future cultural work, I plan to identify the specific language and context involved, consult reliable sources, and explain the limits of my interpretation. This artifact reminds me that cultural understanding grows through study, humility, and continued engagement. Those habits also matter to my goal of improving my Spanish before law school so I can practice law with Spanish-speaking people.

Context sources: Smithsonian NMAI, “Reading the Calendar Glyphs”; Smithsonian NMAI, “The Maya”; British Museum via Smarthistory, “Maya glyphs”.
